# How Foundry Contractors Cut Crew-Scheduling Time by 60% Across Eight Megaprojects

When Foundry Contractors took on the Phoenix Light Rail Extension in 2024, their scheduling lead, Carla Mendez, was managing crew assignments across six other active projects using a combination of spreadsheets, a six-year-old project-management tool, and a lot of late nights. Each Monday she'd spend three to four hours just figuring out who was on which job site that week — and by Wednesday, the schedule had already changed twice.

"We weren't bad at planning," Carla says. "We were bad at *re-planning*. Every time a subcontractor delivery slipped or a permit came through late, I had to rebuild the next two weeks of crew assignments by hand. Across eight projects, that was a full day a week — and that's just the scheduling, before you count the calls to foremen explaining the changes."

When Foundry brought Bridgit Bench into their workflow in early 2025, the goal wasn't to "transform their tech stack" — it was to give Carla and her team back the hours they were losing to manual re-planning.

## The problem with multi-project scheduling

Most workforce-planning tools work fine for a single project. They show you a Gantt chart of crew assignments, let you swap people between roles, and produce a forecast for the next month. Where they fall over is at the eight-projects-at-once level, where:

1. The same person is qualified for multiple roles across multiple sites
2. Changes on one project ripple into three others
3. Forecast accuracy degrades fast when you can't see the cross-project picture
4. Field foremen need a clear view of "who's on my job next week" that's always up to date

Carla's team had been patching these gaps manually — a master spreadsheet she'd update late Sunday nights, a Slack channel for foremen to ask "wait, where's Mike on Tuesday?", and a recurring Monday call where she walked the whole field-ops leadership through the changes.

## What changed when Foundry rolled out Bridgit Bench

The rollout was deliberate — Foundry started with two projects, not all eight, and onboarded foremen in cohorts so they could absorb the change without losing field productivity. By month four, every active project was on Bench, and Carla's Monday workload had compressed from a full day to about 90 minutes.

The shifts that produced the time savings:

- **One source of truth for crew assignments.** When a foreman wanted to know who was on his site next Tuesday, he opened Bench instead of pinging Carla. The Slack channel for "wait, where's Mike?" went quiet within a month.
- **Cross-project visibility for re-planning.** When a subcontractor delivery slipped, Carla could see which other projects would feel the ripple effect and adjust crews proactively instead of reactively.
- **Forecast that updated automatically.** The 60-day headcount forecast that used to take Carla three hours to assemble every Friday is now generated continuously from the live schedule.
- **Foreman trust.** Field leaders stopped second-guessing the schedule because they could see it changing in real time, with reasons attached.

Six months in, Foundry measured the impact: Carla's weekly scheduling time dropped from approximately 22 hours to 9 hours — a 60% reduction. Foundry's VP of Field Operations, Marcus Trent, used the time saved to onboard two additional regional projects without adding scheduling headcount.

## What we learned from Foundry's rollout

The Foundry rollout taught us three things that we now apply to every multi-project workforce-planning engagement:

**Start with a single project, not the whole portfolio.** Foundry's instinct was to roll Bench out across all eight active projects at once. We pushed back and recommended a two-project pilot. The result was a cleaner adoption curve and zero field disruption during the transition.

**Foremen are the second customer.** The scheduling lead is the buyer. The foreman is the daily user. If foremen don't trust the tool, the scheduler stops trusting the tool, and you're back to spreadsheets within a quarter. Foundry's success came from a deliberate foreman-onboarding program that ran in parallel to Carla's adoption.

**Measure time, not just satisfaction.** A lot of workforce-planning rollouts measure success in terms of user-survey responses ("how satisfied are you with Bench?"). That's noise. The number that matters is the hours per week the scheduling team gets back — that's what determines whether the project survives the next budget review.
